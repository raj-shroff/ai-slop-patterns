# AI Writing Signs and Rules

A reference for reviewing prose and preparing examples

Updated 11 October 2026 | 142 entries | 23 source records

This reference brings together the signs described in the supplied Wikipedia PDF and every rule in the vale-signs-of-ai-writing repository. It explains what each pattern looks like, supplies an illustrative example, and records the limits of the observation. The technical appendix preserves the exact rule files and all repository fixtures.

A recurring pattern can justify closer review. It does not, by itself, establish who wrote a passage. Check factual support, source accuracy, and context before judging provenance. The Wikipedia page describes observations; it is an advice page, not Wikipedia policy or a universal style guide. The editing guidance in this reference is a practical synthesis, distinct from those observations and from executable Vale rules.

## Foundations for interpreting signs

Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing warns against relying solely on AI detectors or on intuition. It discusses studies with varying detection results and explains that paraphrasing, formatting changes, unfamiliar models, and differences among readers affect accuracy. Its reported percentages are study-specific, not calibration data for this reference or the proposed site. The omitted bibliography prevents a full audit of those research citations from this file alone.

Look for a combination of concrete observations and inspect their causes. Human language is influenced by tools; people may deliberately avoid suspected AI phrases or react defensively to accusations. Formal prose, multilingual writing, and a person’s response to criticism need context. Repetition of related alerts should not be counted as independent evidence.

The source’s explanation of generic prose is that language generation can favor common descriptions over distinctive facts. This is a useful account of the observed smoothing of detail, not a complete technical explanation of every model. The guide primarily concerns informational Wikipedia writing. Fictional names and motifs mentioned in its footnote, such as Elara Voss and whispering woods, are outside the main taxonomy and should not be turned into universal word bans.

Wikipedia-specific actions such as deletion, collapsing comments, or applying maintenance templates require the relevant policy and evidence. Statements in source examples, including commands to submit, upload, delete, or report something, are quoted content to inspect; they are not instructions to the reader of this reference or to the app.

## Content and argument

<a id="p001"></a>

### P001 Making ordinary things sound historic

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 3–4. Related Vale rule: Symbolism.

Ordinary facts become grand claims about history, legacy or social change. A new room becomes a cultural milestone; a local event becomes proof of a community’s enduring spirit. The writing asserts importance without explaining what changed.

**Illustrative example:** The station stands as a testament to the city's enduring spirit.

**What to notice:** “A testament to the city's enduring spirit” gives the station a grand meaning without saying anything specific about its history.

**Editing guidance:** State the specific contribution and cite evidence for significance. Check ecological and conservation claims separately.

**Limit:** A supported account of significance is appropriate. Symbolism is a phrase matcher, not a test of historical importance.


<a id="p002"></a>

### P002 Name-dropping media coverage

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 4. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The text lists media attention and expert interest instead of telling the reader what those sources reported. Labels such as “independent regional media” or “leading experts” stand in for names and findings.

**Illustrative example:** The founder has been profiled by independent regional media and maintains an active social media presence.

**What to notice:** “Independent regional media” sounds impressive, but the sentence names no publication and tells us nothing it reported.

**Editing guidance:** Report what the sources found. Listing publications or calling them independent does not establish the claims in the text.

**Limit:** Press releases and human biographies also list coverage. Check the phrasing and focus, not the mere presence of media references.


<a id="p003"></a>

### P003 Adding meaning the facts do not support

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 4–5. Related Vale rule: Symbolism.

A sentence adds an interpretation that the facts do not support, often after words such as “highlighting,” “reflecting” or “fostering.” A real source may be cited even though it never made that argument.

**Illustrative example:** The museum added a room, highlighting its profound commitment to cultural access.

**What to notice:** Adding a room does not, by itself, demonstrate a “profound commitment.” The sentence attaches that conclusion without explaining it.

**Editing guidance:** Remove the interpretation or support it with a source that actually makes that claim.

**Limit:** A participial clause can state a real causal result. Verify the relationship rather than banning -ing words.


<a id="p004"></a>

### P004 Promotional language

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 5. Related Vale rule: Vocabulary.

A description sounds like a travel brochure or press release. Places, products and organizations receive sweeping praise instead of specific facts.

**Illustrative example:** Nestled in the heart of the valley, the town boasts a vibrant cultural tapestry.

**What to notice:** “Nestled in the heart” and “vibrant cultural tapestry” could describe hundreds of towns. Neither tells us what this place is like.

**Editing guidance:** Replace praise with concrete, relevant facts. Compare the original and revised text for newly introduced promotion.

**Limit:** Human advertising also uses these phrases. Older model output may be more overtly superlative than newer output.


<a id="p005"></a>

### P005 Vague claims about someone's role

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 6. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Someone is described as “connected with,” “associated with” or “linked to” an activity, but the reader never learns what the person actually did.

**Illustrative example:** In 2017, Mara was associated with leadership at the institute.

**What to notice:** “Was associated with leadership” leaves Mara's role unclear. She could have been a director, adviser or employee.

**Editing guidance:** Name the verified role and dates: for example, Mara directed the institute in 2017, if the source establishes that.

**Limit:** Use qualified wording when the evidence really is uncertain; do not turn a loose association into an invented position.


<a id="p006"></a>

### P006 Unnamed experts and supposed agreement

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 6–7. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Unnamed experts or observers supposedly support a claim. One or two opinions become “broad agreement,” or a short source list is made to sound like part of a much larger consensus.

**Illustrative example:** Experts agree that the project transformed education.

**What to notice:** The sentence does not name the experts or explain what changed in education.

**Editing guidance:** Identify who said what, how many sources support it, and whether the claim represents opinion or evidence.

**Limit:** Some generalizations are well supported. A plural attribution needs plural evidence; such as is misleading only when it implies unsupported additional examples.


<a id="p007"></a>

### P007 The predictable challenges-and-hope ending

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 7. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The ending follows a fixed routine: praise the subject, acknowledge vague challenges, then predict a bright future. “Challenges” and “Future prospects” may appear as separate sections even when there is little to say.

**Illustrative example:** Despite its remarkable success, the center faces challenges. Continued innovation promises a bright future.

**What to notice:** The praise, unnamed challenges and hopeful prediction form a familiar ending. The reader learns neither what the problems are nor how they might be solved.

**Editing guidance:** Describe documented constraints and actual plans, or remove the speculative ending.

**Limit:** A substantive discussion of challenges is useful. The sign is the repeated optimistic template, not the word challenges.


<a id="p008"></a>

### P008 Awards sections with no awards

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 7–8. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A repeated Awards and recognition or Recognition section emphasizes acclaim. Paired X and Y headings can reinforce an outline built around vague prestige.

**Illustrative example:** Awards and recognition: The firm has earned widespread recognition for excellence.

**What to notice:** The heading promises awards, but the sentence supplies only praise. No award or awarding organization is named.

**Editing guidance:** List verified awards with the awarding body and date where relevant; omit empty acclaim.

**Limit:** Human biographies routinely need awards sections. The heading alone is weak evidence.


## Language and grammar

<a id="p009"></a>

### P009 Dense clusters of characteristic vocabulary

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 8–9. Related Vale rule: Vocabulary.

Words such as “pivotal,” “underscores” and “vibrant” crowd into the same passage. The concern is repeated use where simpler or more precise words would do. Favored vocabulary changes between models and over time.

**Illustrative example:** Additionally, the initiative underscores a pivotal interplay within a vibrant landscape.

**What to notice:** “Underscores,” “pivotal,” “interplay,” “vibrant” and “landscape” are crowded into one sentence. Together they make the claim sound weighty while leaving its meaning vague.

**Editing guidance:** Use the most precise ordinary wording, preserve technical meaning, and inspect the whole passage for repeated patterns.

**Limit:** A single word proves nothing. Literal tapestry or underscore may be exact. Do not treat synonyms as equally diagnostic by association.

**Additional examples:**

- **Calling something thoughtful:** The plan takes a thoughtful approach to improving the customer experience.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p010"></a>

### P010 Avoiding simple is and has constructions

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 9–10. Related Vale rules: Symbolism, Vocabulary.

Simple statements use “serves as,” “functions as” or “boasts” where “is” or “has” would do. Calling a thing something it “refers to” can also confuse the subject with its name.

**Illustrative example:** The building serves as a library and boasts four reading rooms.

**What to notice:** “Serves as a library” means that it is a library; “boasts” means that it has reading rooms. The extra wording adds grandeur to a simple fact.

**Editing guidance:** Prefer The building is a library with four reading rooms when that is the intended meaning.

**Limit:** Serves as can accurately describe a temporary function. Has in a perfect tense is different from has meaning possesses.


<a id="p011"></a>

### P011 Negative parallelism adding another quality

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 10–11. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A sentence gives an ordinary description, then upgrades it: “not just X, but Y.” The second half often makes a grander claim without adding evidence.

**Illustrative example:** The archive is not just a collection; it is a gateway to the past.

**What to notice:** “Not just” introduces an ordinary description, then replaces it with the grander “gateway to the past.”

**Editing guidance:** State the substantive point directly unless the contrast answers a real question.

**Limit:** This is common in human persuasion and explanations. Evaluate frequency and whether the correction is needed.


<a id="p012"></a>

### P012 Negative parallelism replacing a quality

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 11. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The writer rejects one description and replaces it with another: “It’s not X. It’s Y.” Repeating this formula makes ordinary points sound like revelations.

**Illustrative example:** This is not a building. It is a movement.

**What to notice:** The two short sentences turn a building into a “movement.” The contrast supplies drama without explaining what makes the building unusual.

**Editing guidance:** Name the actual distinction and its evidence, or remove the rhetorical rejection.

**Limit:** A corrective contrast can be essential when a real misconception exists.

**Additional examples:**

- **More than:** The library is more than a building. It is the beating heart of the community.
- **Instead:** The event did not merely entertain. Instead, it redefined what connection could mean.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p013"></a>

### P013 Reversed negative parallelism

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 11. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The preferred description comes first, followed by a rejected alternative: “Y rather than X.” The contrast can sound decisive even when nobody proposed the alternative.

**Illustrative example:** The policy prioritizes practical consolidation rather than ideological purity.

**What to notice:** “Rather than” sets two descriptions against each other. The sentence sounds decisive, but it does not explain why those are the relevant alternatives.

**Editing guidance:** Retain the comparison only if both alternatives are relevant and supported.

**Limit:** Rather than is normal grammar. The model association is an observation in Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing, not an authorship test.

**Additional examples:**

- **Less like, more like:** The meeting was less like a discussion and more like the beginning of a movement.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p014"></a>

### P014 Confusing a page title with its subject

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 11. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A broad page title is treated as the name of a single thing. A list or topic then acquires intentions, influence or a shared identity it does not have.

**Illustrative example:** List of Coastal Bridges is an important collection that connects communities.

**What to notice:** “List of Coastal Bridges” is a page title. Treating the list itself as something that connects communities confuses the document with the bridges it names.

**Editing guidance:** Introduce the actual topic naturally: This list covers bridges along the coast, if that is its scope.

**Limit:** Some titles can appear naturally in a lead; the defect is artificial reification of the title.


<a id="p015"></a>

### P015 Repetitive groups of three

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 12. Related Vale rule: Lists.

Adjectives, phrases or examples repeatedly arrive in threes, making a thin explanation appear comprehensive.

**Illustrative example:** The program promotes clarity, confidence, and connection through learning, leadership, and legacy.

**What to notice:** There are two sets of three abstract nouns. The neat arrangement makes the program sound comprehensive without describing its activities.

**Editing guidance:** Keep the items that the evidence requires; vary structure according to meaning.

**Limit:** Three real categories can be the right structure. Review whether the items add distinct information.


## Style and formatting

<a id="p016"></a>

### P016 Repeating the page title

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 12–13. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The article repeats its own title as a heading above its content, as if the writer did not account for the platform's existing title.

**Illustrative example:** Harbor Museum<br><br>Harbor Museum<br>The museum opened in 1986.

**What to notice:** “Harbor Museum” appears twice before the first fact. The second heading repeats the page title.

**Example context:** The page title is repeated as the first heading.

**Editing guidance:** Remove the duplicate in a platform that already supplies a title.

**Limit:** A standalone document often needs an explicit title. This sign is platform dependent.


<a id="p017"></a>

### P017 Title case headings

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 13. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Every main word in a heading is capitalized even when the surrounding document uses sentence case.

**Illustrative example:** History And Cultural Significance<br>The school opened in 1910.<br><br>Student life<br>Students organized the first sports club in 1912.

**What to notice:** “History And Cultural Significance” capitalizes every main word, while “Student life” uses sentence case. The inconsistency is visible in the headings.

**Example context:** Notice the switch from title case to sentence case.

**Editing guidance:** Use the capitalization convention of the destination publication.

**Limit:** Title case is normal in many publications and is not generally incorrect.


<a id="p019"></a>

### P019 Too much bold text

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 14. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Bold type is applied to so many words that it stops showing the reader what matters.

**Illustrative example:** Our **strategy** puts **customers** at the center of every **decision**. Through **innovation** and **collaboration**, we deliver **lasting value**.

**What to notice:** So many ordinary words are bold that the emphasis no longer helps the reader find anything important.

**Example context:** The bold words are generic labels; emphasizing them all gives the reader little help.

**Editing guidance:** Use emphasis sparingly and according to the publication's style.

**Limit:** Training materials and business slides may use boldface appropriately. Newer models may be instructed to avoid it.


<a id="p020"></a>

### P020 Lists with a bold label on every line

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 14–15. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Each list item begins with a short label followed by a colon and a description. The same labels recur even when they add little information.

**Illustrative example:** **Efficiency:** The system saves time.<br>**Flexibility:** The system adapts to your needs.<br>**Reliability:** The system delivers consistent results.

**What to notice:** Each line follows the same bold-label-and-claim format. The labels make a thin description look more organized than it is.

**Example context:** Each line uses the same label-and-claim format.

**Editing guidance:** Choose prose or a list based on the information, and use the platform's real list syntax.

**Limit:** Labels are useful in glossaries, instructions and reference lists. Their presence alone is not a writing problem.


<a id="p021"></a>

### P021 Too many em dashes

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 15–16. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Em dashes repeatedly interrupt sentences to add praise, an aside or a dramatic contrast. Some models use this heavily; others use it far less.

**Illustrative example:** The center expanded — a bold step forward — reshaping its future.

**What to notice:** The two dashes interrupt a simple statement to add “a bold step forward.” The praise is doing more work than the facts.

**Editing guidance:** Keep punctuation that clarifies the sentence; reduce repeated rhetorical interruptions when they obscure the claim.

**Limit:** Professional writers use dashes. Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing reports that contemporary models do not uniformly overuse them; this is weak evidence alone.


<a id="p022"></a>

### P022 Emoji as structural decoration

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 16. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Emoji are added to headings or bullets where they do not explain the content or fit the document’s tone.

**Illustrative example:** 🚀 Future prospects: Continued innovation promises a bright future.

**What to notice:** The rocket decorates a heading about future prospects. It adds excitement but no information.

**Editing guidance:** Remove decorative symbols where the destination's style excludes them.

**Limit:** Emoji are ordinary in informal communication. Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing describes this as less common in recent output.


<a id="p023"></a>

### P023 Tables that add no value

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 16. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A table separates a few simple facts into cells without making them easier to compare. Formatting copied from another tool may also display incorrectly.

**Illustrative example:** Name | Harbor Center<br>Type | Museum

**What to notice:** The two rows say only that Harbor Center is a museum. There is no comparison or substantial information for the table to organize.

**Example context:** This two-row table contains information that would fit in one short sentence.

**Editing guidance:** Use a table for meaningful comparisons; use prose for a simple fact and validate markup.

**Limit:** Tables are appropriate for genuinely parallel data. Minimal formatting alone is not evidence.


<a id="p024"></a>

### P024 Mixed quotation mark styles

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 16–17. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A passage switches between straight and curly quotation marks. This is usually a formatting inconsistency, not a useful clue to authorship.

**Illustrative example:** "We’re ready," she said. “It's time to begin.”

**What to notice:** Straight and curly quotation marks appear in the same passage. This often happens when text is copied between editors.

**Example context:** Straight and curly quotation marks are mixed. Copying between editors can cause this.

**Editing guidance:** Normalize typography only if required by house style and preserve quotations accurately.

**Limit:** Word, operating systems, citation tools, and professional typesetting routinely produce curly quotes. Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing says model behavior differs.


<a id="p025"></a>

### P025 Skipped heading levels

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 17. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The document jumps from its main title to a deeply nested subheading without the intermediate section.

**Illustrative example:** # Annual report<br>### Revenue<br>Sales increased in June.

**What to notice:** One # marks a main title in Markdown; three mark a lower-level heading. The example skips the level normally written with ##.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** The heading markup jumps from level one to level three.

**Editing guidance:** Restore a logical heading hierarchy and preview the rendered page.

**Limit:** People also make hierarchy errors. Judge the actual document structure.


<a id="p026"></a>

### P026 Every section looks like a main title

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 17. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Several sections use the top-level title format instead of a consistent hierarchy of section headings.

**Illustrative example:** # History<br>The museum opened in 1986.<br># Results<br>Attendance grew in its first year.<br># Sources<br>Annual report, 1987.

**What to notice:** Every heading starts with a single #, so every section is marked as a main title.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** Every section uses the top heading level, usually reserved for the document title.

**Editing guidance:** Use the destination's section levels and maintain one coherent hierarchy.

**Limit:** Some document formats allow multiple top-level headings. Check the intended structure before treating this as an error.


<a id="p027"></a>

### P027 A divider after every section

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 17. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Horizontal separators are inserted mechanically between sections, often reflecting a Markdown response style.

**Illustrative example:** Background<br>The team met on Tuesday.<br>----<br>Findings<br>The deadline was missed.<br>----<br>Next steps<br>The team will meet again.

**What to notice:** A horizontal line separates each tiny section. The separators give a three-sentence update the appearance of a much larger report.

**Example context:** A separator appears after every short section.

**Editing guidance:** Let headings and spacing separate content unless a rule has a specific purpose.

**Limit:** Rules are legitimate design elements in other contexts and do not establish provenance.


## Communication residue

<a id="p028"></a>

### P028 The chatbot's reply left in the draft

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 18–19. Related Vale rule: ChatbotCommunication.

The finished text still includes the assistant’s reply to the user: greetings, offers to continue, praise, or instructions about submitting the draft.

**Illustrative example:** Certainly! Here is your draft. Delete this note before submitting.

**What to notice:** “Certainly! Here is your draft” addresses the person who requested the text. That reply has been left in the finished document.

**Editing guidance:** Separate the deliverable from correspondence; inspect comments and verify any substantive advice before using it.

**Limit:** A human may quote a chatbot as evidence. Ordinary courteous language in an actual conversation is not itself a defect.


<a id="p029"></a>

### P029 Excuses for missing information

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 19–20. Related Vale rule: KnowledgeCutoff.

The response discusses its training cutoff, missing search results or lack of sources. Sometimes it turns “I could not find this” into “this information is private,” then guesses at a reason.

**Illustrative example:** Details are not publicly available, likely because she maintains a low profile.

**What to notice:** The sentence guesses why information is missing. A failed search does not establish that someone deliberately keeps a low profile.

**Editing guidance:** Describe the specific evidence gap without inventing its cause. Verify the claim or remove the speculation.

**Limit:** A carefully scoped limitation statement is good practice. A statement about missing information should accurately describe what was checked.


<a id="p030"></a>

### P030 Blanks left in the finished text

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 20–21. Related Vale rule: Placeholders.

The finished document still contains blank fields or instructions to the writer, such as [YEAR], [INSERT SOURCE], or a note asking for a photograph.

**Illustrative example:** The organization was founded in [YEAR]. Access date: 2025-xx-xx.

**What to notice:** “[YEAR]” and “2025-xx-xx” are blanks that were never filled in.

**Editing guidance:** Complete fields from evidence, remove inapplicable placeholders, and check embedded comments.

**Limit:** Placeholders belong in unfinished drafts and reusable forms. The problem is leaving them in text presented as complete.


## Markup and tool artifacts

<a id="p031"></a>

### P031 Formatting instructions left in the text

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 21–24. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Formatting instructions appear as ordinary text because the destination does not support the format used by the writing tool. Markdown is a plain-text format that uses symbols for headings, emphasis and links.

**Illustrative example:** ## History<br>The library opened in 1920. [Read the report](report.html)

**What to notice:** The ## symbols and bracketed link instructions are visible to the reader. They were meant to control formatting in an editor that understands Markdown.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** This is what readers see when Markdown is published as plain text.

**Editing guidance:** Translate to the destination's markup and preview the result; inspect the claims as well as syntax.

**Limit:** People also paste text into incompatible editors. Visible formatting code does not establish AI authorship.


<a id="p032"></a>

### P032 Broken code left in a document

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 24. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Copied text contains damaged instructions for a publishing system, such as incomplete labels, repeated dates or error messages inside a submission field.

**Illustrative example:** Review date: 2025-04-012025-04-01ERROR

**What to notice:** The date appears twice, followed by “ERROR.” A publishing or copying mistake has been left in the document.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** A date field contains duplicated text and an error marker.

**Editing guidance:** Compare the field with the publishing system’s documented format and check the rendered result.

**Limit:** Bad markup can come from human errors or tools. Not every unexplained HTML fragment is characteristic of AI.


<a id="p033"></a>

### P033 ChatGPT reference codes

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 24–25. Related Vale rule: ChatGPTArtifacts.

Codes used by ChatGPT to display sources or images sometimes survive when a response is copied. Readers then see strings such as “contentReference” or “turn0search0” instead of a source link.

**Illustrative example:** The result was confirmed :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}.

**What to notice:** The string beginning with “contentReference” is a reference code used by a writing tool. The reader sees that code instead of a usable citation.

**Example format:** code

**Editing guidance:** Recover and verify the underlying source, then create a valid citation. Treat the artifact as evidence of a tool-mediated passage, not a reason to skip factual review.

**Limit:** Quoted examples and technical documentation can contain these strings intentionally. Check whether a marker was accidentally copied or deliberately included as an example.


<a id="p034"></a>

### P034 Gemini citation codes

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 25–26. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A copied response contains labels such as “[cite: 1]” or “[span_1]” where the reader expects a citation or ordinary text.

**Illustrative example:** The harbor opened in 1904.[cite: 3, 12]

**What to notice:** “[cite: 3, 12]” points to numbered sources in the tool's response, but the copied sentence provides no way to open them.

**Example format:** code

**Editing guidance:** Find the actual source and replace the tool marker with an ordinary citation.

**Limit:** Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing associates these forms with Gemini; literal examples in documentation are not accidental artifacts.


<a id="p035"></a>

### P035 Grok citation code

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 26. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Instructions for showing a Grok source card appear in the published sentence. They may include “grok_card” or “grok_render_citation_card_json.”

**Illustrative example:** The harbor opened in 1904. grok_render_citation_card_json

**What to notice:** “grok_render_citation_card_json” is an instruction for displaying a citation. It should not appear as part of an ordinary sentence.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** The tool marker has been left in the published sentence.

**Editing guidance:** Inspect the source behind the card and replace the exposed renderer syntax.

**Limit:** The tag indicates a tool format, not that every surrounding sentence was generated.


<a id="p036"></a>

### P036 Bracketed reference codes

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 26–27. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A copied sentence ends with a bracketed reference number and line numbers. Without the original tool's source list, the reader cannot follow the reference.

**Illustrative example:** The harbor opened in 1904.【85†L261-269】

**What to notice:** The brackets contain a reference number and a range of lines. In this copied sentence, the reader has no source to connect them to.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** A citation marker appears without an accessible reference.

**Editing guidance:** Resolve the cited source and relevant passage; replace internal locators with usable references.

**Limit:** Bracketed notation can be deliberately quoted. Model attribution in Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing is observational.


<a id="p037"></a>

### P037 File labels left in a sentence

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 27. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Labels such as “[attached_file:1]” or “[web:1]” remain in text copied from a writing tool. They refer to a file or web source in that tool, but they are not useful citations on their own.

**Illustrative example:** The report describes the merger.[attached_file:1]

**What to notice:** “[attached_file:1]” is a label for an uploaded file. It has been pasted into the sentence where the reader would expect a normal source link.

**Example format:** code

**Editing guidance:** Use the original document and a stable, authorized reference rather than an internal upload location.

**Limit:** Do not infer public availability from an uploaded file URL, and do not infer authorship of all text from one marker.


<a id="p038"></a>

### P038 Writing-tool code around the text

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 27. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A response has been copied with formatting code still wrapped around it. The code is visible before and after the actual prose.

**Illustrative example:** :::writing{variant="document" id="12345"}<br>The museum opened a new wing in June.<br>:::

**What to notice:** The :::writing line and closing ::: surround the actual sentence. They belong to the writing tool's formatting, not to the article about the museum.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** The export wrapper is visible to the reader.

**Editing guidance:** Remove interface wrappers after checking the enclosed content; preserve them when discussing the format itself.

**Limit:** Do not assign a vendor that the source does not establish.


<a id="p039"></a>

### P039 The wrong category

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 27–28. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The text assigns a page to a publishing category that does not exist or does not fit its subject.

**Illustrative example:** Maya Chen is a jazz pianist who performs with a quartet.<br>Category: American architecture

**What to notice:** A pianist's profile has been filed under architecture. The category does not match the subject.

**Example context:** The category has no connection to the profile.

**Editing guidance:** Check the available category labels and choose one that fits the content.

**Limit:** People also select the wrong category. This is a publishing error, not proof of AI use.


<a id="p040"></a>

### P040 Instructions for fields that do not exist

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 28–29. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The text tells someone to fill in a field that the form does not have.

**Illustrative example:** Enter the year in the historical_population_year field, then select Save.

**What to notice:** In this example, the form has only Name, Address and Telephone fields. The instructions invent a field that the user cannot find.

**Example context:** In this fictional scenario, the form has only Name, Address and Telephone fields.

**Editing guidance:** Check the actual form or its documentation before following the instruction.

**Limit:** Outdated instructions and human mistakes can produce the same error.


## Citations and source checking

<a id="p041"></a>

### P041 Made-up citations

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 29. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Several references in a new contribution lead to nonexistent pages or sites, with no archive evidence that the pages existed.

**Illustrative example:** Sources:<br>https://example.org/research/harbor-cleanup-2024<br>https://example.org/news/port-water-quality-review<br>https://example.org/reports/coastal-recovery-study

**What to notice:** These example.org addresses were made up for this example. Their plausible wording shows how a fabricated source list can look convincing at a glance.

**Example context:** These are deliberately invented example.org addresses. They illustrate plausible-looking citations that do not lead to the claimed publications.

**Editing guidance:** Check publisher search, archives, alternate access, and metadata before concluding that a source is fabricated.

**Limit:** Links decay, institutional access differs, scripts alter URLs, and copying can truncate them.


<a id="p042"></a>

### P042 Invalid book and article identifiers

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 29–30. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A book or article is given an invalid reference number. An ISBN identifies a book edition; a DOI identifies a research publication. A made-up number can look like a legitimate citation.

**Illustrative example:** L. Hart. A History of Harbor Engineering. ISBN 978-0-306-40615-8.

**What to notice:** The book and author are fictional, and the ISBN's final check digit is wrong. A formal-looking book reference still needs to identify a real publication.

**Example context:** The book and author are invented. This ISBN also has an invalid check digit.

**Editing guidance:** Validate the identifier and match it to the cited work; correct from a reliable record.

**Limit:** A typo can cause the same failure. A valid checksum only verifies identifier structure, not the cited claim.


<a id="p043"></a>

### P043 A real source attached to the wrong claim

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 30. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A DOI or other identifier resolves, but its title, authors, date, or subject differ from the cited reference. Apparent citation precision masks fabrication.

**Illustrative example:** “A field study proves the bridge can withstand the flood.” [1]<br>[1] Childhood sleep habits: a survey of school pupils.

**What to notice:** The claim concerns a bridge, but the reference concerns children's sleep. Having a citation does not make the source relevant.

**Example context:** The reference concerns a different subject and cannot support the claim.

**Editing guidance:** Compare full metadata and read the supporting passage. Check chronological impossibilities as well.

**Limit:** Citation tools and people can attach the wrong identifier. A mismatch is a citation defect, not proof of AI authorship.


<a id="p044"></a>

### P044 Book references with no page to check

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 30–31. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A precise claim cites a whole book without giving the page or section needed to check it.

**Illustrative example:** At 24°C, the reaction reaches equilibrium in exactly 18 minutes (Handbook of Industrial Chemistry).

**What to notice:** The precise claim is attributed to an entire handbook. There is no author, edition or page to help the reader check it.

**Example context:** The fictional citation gives no author, edition, chapter or page for a precise claim.

**Editing guidance:** Find the passage and provide a page, chapter, or other suitable locator.

**Limit:** Print books need not have URLs. Missing page numbers are common human citation weaknesses.


<a id="p045"></a>

### P045 Broken citation formatting

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 31–32. Related Vale rule: CitationArtifacts.

References are mechanically appended after every sentence, reused with invalid syntax, or accompanied by footnote return arrows such as ↩ and [↩].

**Illustrative example:** The center opened in 1998. {{cite web...}}<sup>[3]</sup> ↩

**What to notice:** A citation template, an HTML tag and a return arrow have been pasted together. The result looks like unfinished formatting code.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** Several incompatible pieces of citation markup have been pasted together.

**Editing guidance:** Use the publication's citation mechanism and verify that each source supports its claim.

**Limit:** Return arrows are normal footnote navigation on many websites. Their presence in pasted text is context dependent.


<a id="p046"></a>

### P046 AI service names in links

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 32. Related Vale rule: UTMParameters.

Source URLs contain utm_source=chatgpt.com, utm_source=openai, utm_source=copilot.com, or referrer=grok.com. This can indicate how a source was found.

**Illustrative example:** https://example.org/report?utm_source=chatgpt.com

**What to notice:** “utm_source=chatgpt.com” is a tracking label in the address. It records a route to the link; it does not tell us who wrote the surrounding text.

**Editing guidance:** Verify the destination and review revision history to distinguish citation assistance from generated prose.

**Limit:** Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing explicitly says a tracking parameter does not prove the writing was generated. Indexed or recirculated URLs can carry it onward.


<a id="p047"></a>

### P047 References that lead nowhere

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 32–33. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A sentence points to a named reference that was never supplied, or a reference is listed but never used.

**Illustrative example:** The center opened in 1998.<ref name="report" />

**What to notice:** The sentence points to a reference named “report,” but this example never supplies that reference.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** The reference has no corresponding definition in this example.

**Editing guidance:** Pair definitions and uses, remove unused entries where appropriate, and preview citation errors.

**Limit:** Copying part of an article can create the same mismatch.


## Discussion comments

<a id="p048"></a>

### P048 Invented rules and misquoted policies

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 33. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The text claims that a rule or policy supports its position, but the rule does not exist or says something different.

**Illustrative example:** Company policy requires customer complaints to be deleted after 30 days.

**What to notice:** In this fictional case, the company has no such policy. The sentence uses the authority of a rule to support an invented requirement.

**Example context:** In this fictional scenario, the company has no such policy.

**Editing guidance:** Find the policy being cited and check what it actually requires.

**Limit:** People misremember policy. Check the substance before making claims about the writer.


<a id="p049"></a>

### P049 Accidentally inserting an editor's warning

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 33. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Code that creates an editor's warning is pasted into a comment intended to discuss the warning.

**Illustrative example:** Please remove the {{citation needed}} label from the opening sentence.

**What to notice:** The text tries to discuss a “citation needed” label. In an editor that recognizes the curly-bracket command, it could insert the label instead.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** In a system that interprets this markup, the comment inserts a warning while trying to discuss its name.

**Editing guidance:** Quote the label as ordinary text instead of inserting the publishing command.

**Limit:** This requires a publishing system with reusable notices. It is not a general prose pattern, and people can make the same mistake.


<a id="p050"></a>

### P050 Too many sections for a short comment

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 33. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A short message is split into a report-like sequence of headings. The structure adds bulk without helping the reader.

**Illustrative example:** Background<br>You suggested changing the title.<br><br>Analysis<br>The proposed title raises questions of clarity.<br><br>Recommendation<br>We should consider an alternative.<br><br>Conclusion<br>A clear title is important.

**What to notice:** Four headings are used to discuss a small title change. Most of the text repeats that a clear title would be useful.

**Example context:** Four sections are used to say very little about a minor title change.

**Editing guidance:** Use the amount of structure the discussion needs; state the specific issue and evidence.

**Limit:** Complex discussions sometimes benefit from headings and detail.


<a id="p051"></a>

### P051 Claiming the work meets every requirement

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 33. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The response downplays AI involvement by listing policies supposedly satisfied or insisting the words represent the writer's own thoughts.

**Illustrative example:** These are entirely my own views, and the report fully complies with every requirement for accuracy and originality.

**What to notice:** The writer claims complete originality and accuracy but supplies no details about how the report was prepared or checked.

**Editing guidance:** Explain the actual contribution and evidence; follow applicable disclosure practices.

**Limit:** An assurance is neither proof of innocence nor proof of AI. Do not punish a person simply for responding to concern.


<a id="p052"></a>

### P052 Stock requests for feedback

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 33. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A defensive reply asks critics to specify exactly what must be improved, often within a longer canned response.

**Illustrative example:** Please identify the precise changes needed to bring my contribution into full compliance.

**What to notice:** The reply uses formal language to ask what should change. It does not engage with a particular criticism.

**Editing guidance:** Ask or answer a concrete editorial question tied to the disputed content.

**Limit:** Requests for help are normal and should be encouraged; this is weak contextual evidence only.


<a id="p053"></a>

### P053 Dismissing questions about AI use

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 33. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The comment characterizes concerns as unsupported speculation and demands concrete proof, often using a recurring defensive formula.

**Illustrative example:** These allegations rely on speculation rather than concrete evidence.

**What to notice:** The reply dismisses the concern as speculation. It offers no account of how the text was written.

**Editing guidance:** Discuss observable source or content problems and state the limits of any inference.

**Limit:** Writers can reasonably dispute false accusations. The stance alone cannot establish AI use.


<a id="p054"></a>

### P054 Deflecting questions about AI use

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 33–34. Related Vale rule: ChatbotCommunication.

Asked how a passage was produced, the response redirects the conversation toward improving the text without answering the question.

**Illustrative example:** Let us focus on improving the report rather than discussing how it was written.

**What to notice:** The response moves the discussion to improving the report while leaving the question about its origin unanswered.

**Editing guidance:** Answer the question about how the text was produced, then address any requested edits.

**Limit:** Content-focused discussion and polite openings are legitimate. Evaluate the surrounding pattern.


## Edit summaries

<a id="p055"></a>

### P055 Revision notes that say little

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 34–35. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A revision note lists broad improvements without identifying what actually changed.

**Illustrative example:** Improved clarity, flow, readability and overall quality.

**What to notice:** “Clarity,” “flow” and “overall quality” sound positive, but the note does not identify a single edit.

**Editing guidance:** Describe the concrete change and its purpose in proportion to the edit.

**Limit:** A brief summary can be appropriate. The issue is whether it helps the reader understand the revision.


<a id="p056"></a>

### P056 Claims of meeting every standard

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 35–36. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A revision note says the work meets every rule or standard, but never explains what was checked or changed.

**Illustrative example:** Updated the report to ensure full compliance with all relevant standards for accuracy and clarity.

**What to notice:** The note claims “full compliance” without naming a standard or saying what changed in the report.

**Editing guidance:** Name the specific change and relevant policy only when useful.

**Limit:** A concise policy-specific explanation is normal. Generic assurances do not demonstrate compliance.


<a id="p057"></a>

### P057 Listing what stayed the same

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 36–37. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The summary describes what was retained, preserved, avoided, or deliberately left untouched, echoing constraints in a possible editing prompt.

**Illustrative example:** Improved neutrality while preserving structure, references, and all technical details.

**What to notice:** Most of the note describes what was kept. The reader still cannot see which wording changed.

**Editing guidance:** Explain meaningful constraints only when they help a reviewer understand the edit.

**Limit:** Preservation is genuinely important in some revisions. This pattern is contextual, not inherently improper.


<a id="p058"></a>

### P058 Saying sources exist without saying what they show

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 37–38. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The summary stresses sourced, verified, independent, secondary, or peer-reviewed content without saying what information was added.

**Illustrative example:** Expanded the article with verified information and independent sources.

**What to notice:** The note says that sources are verified and independent without identifying the information they support.

**Editing guidance:** Describe the new finding or fact and cite it properly.

**Limit:** Adding or repairing references can itself be the entire edit and should then be stated plainly.


<a id="p059"></a>

### P059 Revision notes buried in formatting details

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 38. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A note about an edit lists tiny formatting changes at such length that the actual revision becomes hard to find.

**Illustrative example:** Adjusted the third heading’s font size, moved two commas, replaced three link labels and realigned the final table cell.

**What to notice:** The small formatting operations overwhelm the note. It becomes hard to tell whether anything important changed.

**Editing guidance:** Include only technical details needed to review the change.

**Limit:** A technical review may need this detail. Include it when it helps someone check the work.


<a id="p060"></a>

### P060 Saying feedback was addressed without explaining how

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 38. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A response to a reviewer says the feedback has been addressed without identifying the concern or explaining the correction.

**Illustrative example:** Addressed reviewer feedback by improving sourcing, formatting, and neutrality.

**What to notice:** The note says the feedback was addressed but does not identify the feedback or the correction.

**Editing guidance:** State which concern was resolved and what evidence or content changed.

**Limit:** Mentioning feedback is normal. The weak sign is the generic formula without useful specifics.


## Context and behavior

<a id="p061"></a>

### P061 A sudden change in writing style

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 39. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A writer’s grammar, tone or variety of English suddenly changes in comparable work. The shift may resemble the style of a particular model, but a new editor or a different task can also explain it.

**Illustrative example:** Earlier update: The pump broke. We ordered a part.<br>Later update: The operational interruption underscores the importance of a comprehensive maintenance strategy.

**What to notice:** The first update describes a broken pump plainly. The second replaces concrete information with formal, abstract language about maintenance.

**Example context:** A sudden change in comparable updates merits a question, not an automatic accusation.

**Editing guidance:** Compare comparable genres and dated revisions; seek an ordinary explanation before inferring tool use.

**Limit:** Editing help, code switching, multilingual writing, collaboration, and deliberate improvement can all explain a shift.


<a id="p062"></a>

### P062 Assurances left in a finished draft

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 39–40. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A finished document includes a note to the reviewer declaring that the work meets every requirement instead of providing the evidence needed to check it.

**Illustrative example:** Reviewer note: This report meets every requirement for originality, accuracy and adequate sourcing.

**What to notice:** The note declares that the report meets every requirement. It gives the reviewer an assurance instead of something to check.

**Editing guidance:** Keep submission notes separate from the document and address the actual review criteria.

**Limit:** A required cover note is legitimate. A declaration of compliance does not establish whether the requirements were met.


<a id="p063"></a>

### P063 A review label added too soon

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 40. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A draft arrives with a status such as “reviewed” or “rejected” even though the corresponding review has not happened. The label may have been copied from an example.

**Illustrative example:** Quarterly report — first draft<br>Status: Approved by the review committee

**What to notice:** The fictional report is marked “Approved” before the review committee has received it.

**Example context:** In this fictional scenario, the committee has not received the draft.

**Editing guidance:** Check the review history and use the actual status of the document.

**Limit:** Some systems add status labels automatically. Check how the label was inserted before drawing a conclusion.


<a id="p064"></a>

### P064 Profiles that all sound the same

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 40–41. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A personal profile follows a familiar set of welcome, interests and contribution headings, with little personal information.

**Illustrative example:** Welcome To My User Page! About Me. My Contributions. Let's Connect!

**What to notice:** The profile uses a sequence of familiar welcome and biography headings without saying anything about the person.

**Editing guidance:** Use a personal description appropriate to the platform; inspect the combination of copied formatting and formulaic content.

**Limit:** Many people choose similar profile sections without AI.


<a id="p065"></a>

### P065 The same filler added to unrelated pages

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 41–42. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Repeated generic additions across unrelated pages may accompany attempts to build an account’s edit count. The text and the account’s behavior need separate checks.

**Illustrative example:** Museum page: This institution plays a vital role in the community.<br>Bridge page: This structure plays a vital role in the community.<br>Festival page: This event plays a vital role in the community.

**What to notice:** The same praise appears on three unrelated pages, with only the subject changed.

**Example context:** The same generic addition appears on unrelated pages. Assess any account behavior separately.

**Editing guidance:** Review the actual edits and evidence of gaming separately.

**Limit:** Rapid AI-assisted contributions do not, by themselves, establish permissions gaming or malicious intent.


<a id="p066"></a>

### P066 Writing habits change between models

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 42. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Writing habits differ between models and change after updates. A punctuation habit or favorite phrase associated with one version may disappear in the next.

**Illustrative example:** Version A: The project is a milestone—a testament to collaboration.<br>Version B: The project is more than a milestone. It is a testament to collaboration.

**What to notice:** The second version removes the dash but keeps the exaggerated praise. A change in punctuation does not necessarily change the underlying style.

**Example context:** Two invented versions show how a familiar claim can survive a change in punctuation.

**Editing guidance:** Treat each pattern as conditional on period, model, prompt, and genre.

**Limit:** This is interpretive context, not a separate diagnostic rule or a reliable model classifier.

**Additional examples:**

- **New punctuation, familiar praise:** The library is more than a building. It is a living testament to the community’s aspirations.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p067"></a>

### P067 Repeating an official position as fact

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 42. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The writing repeats a government's preferred account without examining its claims or acknowledging relevant criticism.

**Illustrative example:** The government’s reforms delivered prosperity and demonstrated the wisdom of its leadership.

**What to notice:** The sentence praises the government as if the conclusion were settled. It names no results, evidence or competing views.

**Example context:** An unsupported endorsement leaves no room for contrary evidence. Human writers can use the same framing.

**Editing guidance:** Check source diversity, framing, omissions, and evidence directly.

**Limit:** Political bias occurs in human writing too. The research summarized in Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing is not a universal claim about all models or current versions.


## Human context and ineffective indicators

<a id="p068"></a>

### P068 Writing that predates the chatbot

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 42–43. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A dated copy shows that the text existed before the particular chatbot was available. This can rule out that chatbot as the source of the original passage.

**Illustrative example:** The garden is a vibrant tapestry of color and life.

**What to notice:** If this sentence appears in a verified 2018 copy, it cannot have originated in a chatbot released later. Familiar wording cannot override the date.

**Example context:** Imagine this exact sentence appears in a verified 2018 archive. Its wording cannot establish use of a chatbot released later.

**Editing guidance:** Check an archived copy, publication date or document version history.

**Limit:** Earlier text-generation tools existed. A date predating one chatbot does not prove that no automated writing tool was involved.


<a id="p069"></a>

### P069 A specific explanation of an editing mistake

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 43. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A writer can give a coherent account of a mistake, supply the real source, or explain how a bad URL arose.

**Illustrative example:** I typed 2019 instead of 2018. The date is on page 12 of the annual report; I have corrected it.

**What to notice:** The writer identifies the mistake, gives a page number and explains the correction. These details give a reviewer something concrete to verify.

**Example context:** A specific explanation gives the reviewer something to check.

**Editing guidance:** Invite a concrete explanation and verify it against the evidence.

**Limit:** A good explanation supports a human-error account but is not an infallible authorship test.


<a id="p070"></a>

### P070 Ordinary wording is not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 43. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Ordinary verbs such as is, has, wrote and used, and qualifications such as perhaps or tends to, occur in both human and generated writing. They are not useful authorship tests by themselves.

**Illustrative example:** The report says there is a delay and that the team used a temporary pump.

**What to notice:** “Says,” “is” and “used” are ordinary words. Their presence does not distinguish a person's writing from a model's.

**Editing guidance:** Write for meaning rather than removing every hedge, superlative, or ordinary phrase.

**Limit:** These are comparative observations in the source, not instructions to insert imperfections or proof that such sentences are human.


<a id="p071"></a>

### P071 Correct grammar is not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 43. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Correct grammar alone does not distinguish AI from skilled or professionally edited writing.

**Illustrative example:** The bus arrived at nine. Three passengers boarded, and the driver closed the doors.

**What to notice:** The sentences are grammatically correct. That is a normal feature of edited human writing.

**Example context:** Correct grammar is ordinary. It is not an AI tell.

**Editing guidance:** Judge factual support and distinctive patterns, not proficiency itself.

**Limit:** A dramatic unexplained change in a comparable writing history is a different contextual question.


<a id="p072"></a>

### P072 Mixed formal and casual language is not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 43. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A passage mixes technical language with emotion or casual speech. People do this naturally; the mixture alone does not identify AI use.

**Illustrative example:** The pressure dropped below the operating threshold. So, yes, the pump had a pretty bad afternoon.

**What to notice:** The technical description is followed by a casual joke. People regularly switch tone this way.

**Example context:** Technical language and a casual aside can coexist in human writing.

**Editing guidance:** Consider genre, authorship history, and collaboration before drawing an inference.

**Limit:** Do not use a person's communication style or possible neurodivergence as a proxy for AI use.


<a id="p073"></a>

### P073 Dry prose is not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 43–44. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Plain or dull writing is not evidence of AI use. Look for a specific problem rather than treating a general impression of blandness as a finding.

**Illustrative example:** The office opens at nine and closes at five. Visitors must sign in at reception.

**What to notice:** The passage is dry because it gives opening hours and a visitor rule. Plain prose can suit the task.

**Example context:** Plain, dry prose can be exactly what the reader needs.

**Editing guidance:** Name an observable content or language issue rather than relying on a feeling.

**Limit:** Generated writing can be positive and verbose; human reference prose can be deliberately plain.


<a id="p074"></a>

### P074 Formal language is not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 44. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Academic and technical writing often needs formal vocabulary. The overuse of a particular word does not make every sophisticated word suspicious.

**Illustrative example:** The sample showed a statistically significant increase in mean arterial pressure.

**What to notice:** “Statistically significant” and “mean arterial pressure” have precise meanings in medical research. Formal vocabulary is appropriate here.

**Example context:** Technical vocabulary is appropriate in a medical research report.

**Editing guidance:** Check whether the terms are accurate and necessary.

**Limit:** Expert human writing can be formal, complex, and polished.


<a id="p075"></a>

### P075 One transition is not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 44. Related Vale rule: Transitions.

Words such as however or consequently occur routinely in human writing. Formulaic repetition of certain transitions is a different claim.

**Illustrative example:** However, the second trial failed.

**What to notice:** “However” signals a change in the result. A single useful transition is ordinary writing.

**Editing guidance:** Keep a transition that accurately expresses the relationship.

**Limit:** A single transition is not evidence of AI authorship. Check whether the connection between the statements makes sense.


<a id="p076"></a>

### P076 Missing citations are not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 44. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

People wrote unsupported claims long before modern chatbots, and generated text can include citations. Missing references alone do not identify the author.

**Illustrative example:** The town’s population doubled in five years.

**What to notice:** The population claim needs a source. Its missing citation tells us nothing about whether a person or a model wrote it.

**Example context:** The claim needs a source. That omission alone tells us nothing about who wrote it.

**Editing guidance:** Request or locate support for the claims regardless of provenance.

**Limit:** The presence of citations is no guarantee either; check their existence and relevance.


<a id="p077"></a>

### P077 Broken formatting is not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 44. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Unexpected formatting commands, misplaced italic markers or stray code can come from a browser extension, translation tool or document editor.

**Illustrative example:** I borrowed *The Wind in the Willow*s from the library.

**What to notice:** The asterisk closes in the wrong place and splits the book title. Copying or editing mistakes can produce this.

**Example format:** code

**Example context:** The closing emphasis marker is misplaced.

**Editing guidance:** Check how the text was copied or converted and fix the formatting error.

**Limit:** Distinguish a recognizable tool artifact from an arbitrary formatting error.


<a id="p078"></a>

### P078 Correct formatting is not an AI tell

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 44. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Correctly formatted documents can result from templates, visual editing tools or ordinary experience.

**Illustrative example:** ## Results<br>The second trial finished on time.<br><br>- Samples tested: 24<br>- Samples excluded: 2

**What to notice:** The heading and bullets are formatted correctly. A template or an experienced writer can produce the same result.

**Example context:** Correct headings and lists can be produced by either a person or a model.

**Editing guidance:** Assess content and source quality directly.

**Limit:** Correct formatting establishes neither human nor AI authorship.


## Historical indicators

<a id="p079"></a>

### P079 Telling readers what to note

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 44–45. Related Vale rules: Narrative, Hedging.

The text repeatedly tells readers that something is “important to note” or “worth remembering” before making a simple point. This was common in early chatbot responses.

**Illustrative example:** It is important to note that requirements may vary.

**What to notice:** “It is important to note” delays the simple point that requirements vary.

**Editing guidance:** State the actual limitation or difference and provide the relevant context.

**Limit:** An explicit qualification may be necessary. This observation concerns earlier chatbot output and should not be assumed to apply to every current model.


<a id="p080"></a>

### P080 Repetitive section summaries

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 45. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

Older long-form output ends sections by restating the same point, often under Conclusion or with In summary, In conclusion, or Overall.

**Illustrative example:** The new timetable gives the team an extra week to prepare. This additional preparation time will help the team. In summary, the team will benefit from having more time to prepare.

**What to notice:** All three sentences say that extra preparation time helps the team. “In summary” introduces another repetition.

**Example context:** The final two sentences repeat the first.

**Editing guidance:** Keep a summary when it synthesizes something useful; remove mere repetition.

**Limit:** Human reports and teaching materials legitimately use conclusions.


<a id="p081"></a>

### P081 The chatbot identifies itself or refuses to answer

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 45–46. Related Vale rule: ChatbotCommunication.

A copied response apologizes, identifies itself as a language model, refuses a request, or offers an alternative task.

**Illustrative example:** As an AI language model, I cannot do that, but I can draft an outline.

**What to notice:** The response explicitly calls itself an AI language model. The assistant's refusal has been copied into the text.

**Editing guidance:** Exclude correspondence from the article and inspect any replacement content independently.

**Limit:** A quotation about AI behavior may intentionally contain this wording; historical frequency is not a current guarantee.


<a id="p082"></a>

### P082 Abrupt cutoffs

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 46. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

A response stops mid-thought, historically associated with output-length limits and a continue-generating interaction.

**Illustrative example:** The repairs will begin on Monday. The remaining work includes replacing the

**What to notice:** The sentence ends after “replacing the.” The object of the sentence is missing.

**Example context:** The passage stops in the middle of a sentence.

**Editing guidance:** Recover the missing source text or remove the incomplete claim after checking context.

**Limit:** Broken copying or an incomplete human draft can produce the same result; the source also mentions possible copying from copyrighted material.


<a id="p083"></a>

### P083 Old dates in newly added references

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 46. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

New references carry much older dates for when the writer supposedly checked them.

**Illustrative example:** Report submitted: 12 May 2025<br>Source accessed: 8 March 2024<br>Second source accessed: 8 March 2024

**What to notice:** Both access dates are much older than the report. That is a reason to check the references, although old dates can be accurate.

**Example context:** The dates may be genuine, but repeated old access dates are worth checking.

**Editing guidance:** Check how the references were obtained and whether the dates are genuine.

**Limit:** Copied references, offline work, merges, and batch edits legitimately retain earlier dates.


<a id="p084"></a>

### P084 Elegant variation and forced synonyms

Source: Wikipedia PDF pp. 46–47. Related Vale rules: none directly implemented.

The same person, place or thing gets a new label in each sentence. The variety can make readers wonder whether the writer is still discussing the same subject.

**Illustrative example:** The laboratory opened in June. The research hub welcomed its first students in July. By August, the scientific center was preparing its first exhibition.

**What to notice:** “Laboratory,” “research hub” and “scientific center” all name the same place. The repeated renaming makes that harder to follow.

**Example context:** Three labels refer to the same laboratory.

**Editing guidance:** Use stable names and precise references when readers need continuity.

**Limit:** Some educational traditions discourage repetition. Separate generation sessions may not show the pattern.


## Additional repository rules

<a id="p085"></a>

### P085 Vague talk about aspects and dimensions

Source: Repository configuration; see the rule register. Related Vale rule: AspectOveruse.

Broad phrases such as “various aspects,” “many dimensions” or “multifaceted” stand in for naming the actual issues.

**Illustrative example:** We need to examine the various aspects of the design.

**What to notice:** “Various aspects” does not identify what needs attention in the design.

**Editing guidance:** Name the actual components or questions when possible.

**Limit:** Some broad descriptions are useful. The problem is leaving the relevant details unspecified.


<a id="p086"></a>

### P086 Vague claims of being among the first or best

Source: Repository configuration; see the rule register. Related Vale rule: Enumeration.

Phrases such as “one of the most” or “among the earliest” imply a ranking without naming the comparison group.

**Illustrative example:** The device was one of the first of its kind.

**What to notice:** “One of the first” suggests a ranking without naming earlier devices or the group being compared.

**Editing guidance:** Specify a supported comparison, scope, and date, or avoid the ranking.

**Limit:** A qualified ranking can be accurate. Check its scope and supporting evidence.


<a id="p087"></a>

### P087 Announcing importance instead of getting to the point

Source: Repository configuration; see the rule register. Related Vale rule: Hedging.

Phrases such as “it is worth noting,” “clearly” and “without a doubt” repeatedly announce importance or certainty instead of supplying evidence.

**Illustrative example:** It is worth noting the result. Clearly, it matters. Without a doubt, it will endure.

**What to notice:** Each sentence announces importance or certainty. None supplies evidence for the claim.

**Editing guidance:** Delete empty preambles, state uncertainty accurately, and avoid unsupported certainty.

**Limit:** An introductory phrase can help a reader. Review whether repeated assertions of certainty are supported.


<a id="p088"></a>

### P088 Emphasis without specifics

Source: Repository configuration; see the rule register. Related Vale rule: Intensifiers.

Words such as “notably,” “particularly” and “exceptionally” add emphasis without necessarily explaining the size or importance of a result.

**Illustrative example:** The performance was exceptionally strong.

**What to notice:** “Exceptionally strong” praises the performance without saying how it was measured or what it was compared with.

**Editing guidance:** Replace unsupported emphasis with a measure or a precise description.

**Limit:** An intensifier may be precise in context. “Statistically significant,” for example, has a technical meaning.

**Additional examples:**

- **Every single:** Every single detail reflects our commitment to excellence.
- **Enormously:** The team benefited enormously from the new approach.
- **Matters enormously:** The choice of wording matters enormously for the future of the organization.
- **An enormous amount:** The meeting generated an enormous amount of valuable insight.
- **Remarkably:** The new approach is remarkably effective.
- **Incredibly:** The workshop was incredibly valuable for everyone involved.
- **Absolutely essential:** A shared vision is absolutely essential to lasting success.
- **Immense:** The initiative has immense potential to transform the community.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p089"></a>

### P089 Passive voice that hides the actor

Source: Repository configuration; see the rule register. Related Vale rule: Passive.

Sentences repeatedly describe what was done without naming who did it. Phrases such as “was decided,” “was established” and “is considered” can leave responsibility unclear.

**Illustrative example:** The deadline was changed. The request was reviewed, and it was decided that further action was not required.

**What to notice:** The deadline changes and a decision is made, but no person or team is named as responsible.

**Editing guidance:** Choose active or passive voice based on emphasis, agency, and clarity.

**Limit:** Passive voice is often useful. Review whether it obscures responsibility rather than treating it as an authorship signal.


<a id="p090"></a>

### P090 Doubled-up skepticism

Source: Repository configuration; see the rule register. Related Vale rule: ScareQuotes.

The phrase “so-called” is followed by quotation marks that add another layer of skepticism without explaining the criticism.

**Illustrative example:** The company uses a so-called 'innovative' approach.

**What to notice:** “So-called” already signals doubt. The quotation marks around “innovative” repeat that signal without explaining the objection.

**Editing guidance:** Explain the criticism and attribution directly, or retain accurately justified distancing.

**Limit:** Quotation marks can legitimately signal a disputed term. Make the basis for the skepticism clear.


<a id="p091"></a>

### P091 A colon where the sentence should continue

Source: Repository configuration; see the rule register. Related Vale rule: ColonOveruse.

A colon appears before an introductory phrase such as “including,” “such as” or “for example,” interrupting a sentence that could run directly into the examples.

**Illustrative example:** The service has benefits: including faster processing.

**What to notice:** The colon breaks the sentence before “including,” even though the examples could follow directly.

**Editing guidance:** Write The benefits include faster processing, or introduce a proper list after the colon.

**Limit:** Colons are useful for introducing lists and explanations. Check the sentence rather than banning the punctuation.


<a id="p092"></a>

### P092 First, second and third without a reason

Source: Repository configuration; see the rule register. Related Vale rule: Lists.

A paragraph marches through “first,” “second” and “third” even when its points do not need to be presented as a sequence.

**Illustrative example:** First, the park is a welcoming space. Second, it brings people together. Third, it builds a sense of community.

**What to notice:** The numbered transitions separate three versions of the same broad point. They do not describe steps or a meaningful order.

**Example context:** These overlapping claims are presented as a sequence without a clear reason.

**Editing guidance:** Keep ordered instructions when the sequence matters; otherwise avoid a mechanical outline.

**Limit:** Ordered steps are appropriate for instructions and structured arguments. The wording alone does not identify AI use.

## Complete Vale rule register

All 18 source YAML files follow, each paired with its entire fixture file. The raw files are the authority for exact spelling, escaping, punctuation, alternatives, and configuration. There are 17 existence rules and one occurrence rule. The declared levels are five errors, four warnings, and nine suggestions. Only Lists declares paragraph scope. Only Lists and Passive explicitly declare ignorecase: true. Only ChatGPTArtifacts, CitationArtifacts, and UTMParameters explicitly declare nonword: true. ## Differences that affect interpretation

### Individual words versus density

Vocabulary, Transitions, and Intensifiers are existence checks. Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing emphasizes clusters, repetition, genre, and literal meaning. A single alert does not implement that contextual judgment.

### Hedges and rankings

The repository flags qualified rankings and some intensifiers. Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing’s human-syntax section also lists definitive or superlative constructions, hedging qualifiers, and intensifiers as relatively human-associated. These statements concern different contexts and cannot be merged into a universal ban.

### Rhetorical threes versus ordered steps

Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing’s rule of three is broader than Lists.yml. A three-adjective phrase may fit Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing observation without matching the rule; useful first-second-third instructions may match the rule without being poor writing.

### Historical patterns versus current use

Didactic language, summaries, refusals, cutoffs, old access dates, and elegant variation are grouped historically in Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing. Em dashes have an explicit caution about changing model behavior. The YAML rules do not encode era.

### Tool involvement versus generated prose

A service URL or tracking parameter can show a citation workflow without establishing who wrote the passage. Internal markup is stronger evidence of copied tool output, but quoted documentation is a legitimate exception.

### Names versus implementation

AspectOveruse and ColonOveruse have no overuse threshold. Passive is a narrow regex family. Hedging includes certainty phrases. Interpret the code, not just the filename.

### Fixtures versus executable guarantees

Symbolism’s serves-as pattern conflicts with a negative example; CitationArtifacts has a positive bare-arrow example not covered by its digit-requiring arrow pattern. Several lower-case rules have capitalized fixtures without explicit ignorecase. Punctuation and Markdown parsing introduce additional questions. These require a runtime test before claiming exact lint behavior.

### Missing automated coverage

The package does not directly implement most PDF categories, including negative parallelisms, media-notability formulas, vague attribution, heading hierarchy, model-specific artifacts beyond selected ChatGPT forms, source verification, comment behavior, edit-summary formulas, and human counterindicators.

### General writing versus Wikipedia conventions

Markdown, title case, bold labels, tables, headings, and profile sections can be appropriate in other settings. Client presentations and student writing should be judged against their own purpose and format.

### Confidence versus severity

The source’s error/warning/suggestion labels do not supply confidence scores. The app should neither invent a percent-AI score nor add overlapping alerts as if they were independent evidence.

## Practical writing and review rules

These are editorial recommendations synthesized from the sources. They are not additional claims that a particular wording proves AI use.

1. Start with the claim. Identify exactly what the sentence asserts and what a reader would need to verify it.

1. Replace generic praise with specific information. Do not add dates, numbers, roles, or causal claims merely to make a rewrite sound concrete.

1. Name the source of an opinion. Check whether the source says what the passage attributes to it, and whether a claimed consensus has adequate support.

1. Keep meaningful uncertainty. Remove empty caveats, but do not replace a qualified finding with unjustified certainty.

1. Use ordinary verbs when they express the intended meaning. Keep technical vocabulary, passive voice, or a contrast when they improve precision.

1. Let structure follow the material. Use lists, tables, headings, and summaries where they help readers; avoid repeating a template regardless of content.

1. Separate correspondence and drafting notes from the finished text. Check comments, placeholders, citation fields, and output wrappers before publication.

1. Verify references beyond whether the link opens. Match author, title, date, identifier, and the passage supporting the claim; use a locator for long sources.

1. Fix the underlying issue before polishing the surface. Removing a stock phrase does not repair an invented citation or unsupported argument.

1. Review the destination format. Preview markup, test headings and references, and distinguish normal platform conventions from accidental artifacts.

1. Check context before discussing authorship. Compare revision dates and comparable writing samples, consider legitimate tools, and ask for an explanation of concrete errors.

1. Keep the inference proportional. Record observations and uncertainty; do not convert style judgments into accusations or unvalidated detector scores.

## Additional canonical patterns

<a id="p093"></a>

### P093 — Repeated sentence templates

Source records: S05,S07,S09,S11,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Several sentences begin the same way or use the same grammatical shape. The repetition sounds automatic rather than deliberate.

**Illustrative example:** We tested the queue. We tested the cache. We tested the logs. Every test became a lesson. Every lesson became a change.

**What to notice:** The repeated “We tested” opening is followed by a chain of “Every…” sentences. The repeated structure becomes more noticeable than the information.

**Editing guidance:** Combine related results or vary the structure to reflect their relationships.

**Limit:** Repetition can be deliberate rhetoric or necessary parallel reporting.


<a id="p094"></a>

### P094 Every sentence has the same rhythm

Source records: S05,S07,S09. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Passages have nearly identical sentence lengths, equally sized paragraphs or a predictable long-short sequence.

**Illustrative example:** The new system helps the team complete its work. The updated schedule gives each employee more planning time. The weekly meeting keeps every department informed of progress. The shared dashboard makes the latest figures readily available.

**What to notice:** The sentences are almost the same length and follow similar shapes. Read together, they have an unusually even rhythm.

**Example context:** Four sentences each contain nine or ten words and follow a similar grammatical shape.

**Editing guidance:** Give each idea the space it needs; read the passage aloud.

**Limit:** Uniformity also comes from templates, accessibility requirements and human editing.

**Additional examples:**

- **Similar sentence lengths:** The first meeting gave the team a clear direction. The second meeting helped the team review its progress. The third meeting allowed the team to discuss changes.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p095"></a>

### P095 — Dramatic fragments and slogan endings

Source records: S07. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Ordinary observations are split into tiny statements or every paragraph ends with a polished maxim.

**Illustrative example:** One decision. One deadline. One chance. That is leadership.

**What to notice:** The short fragments turn an ordinary decision into a dramatic slogan about leadership.

**Editing guidance:** Join connected thoughts and retain emphasis for the actual turning point.

**Limit:** Fragments are legitimate in dialogue, advertising and speeches.


<a id="p096"></a>

### P096 — Negative chains before a reveal

Source records: S07,S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A series of denials delays the positive claim: no X, no Y; did not X, did not Y.

**Illustrative example:** No framework. No playbook. No magic. Just a spreadsheet.

**What to notice:** Three things are ruled out before the spreadsheet is revealed. The buildup makes a simple answer sound momentous.

**Editing guidance:** State what was used and explain any relevant exclusions.

**Limit:** A list of exclusions can be essential to define scope.


<a id="p097"></a>

### P097 — Staged revelations

Source records: S07,S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The writer promises a hidden truth, a twist or an overlooked detail, then delivers an ordinary observation.

**Illustrative example:** Here is the part nobody mentions: the report needs an owner.

**What to notice:** The introduction promises an overlooked insight. The reveal is the ordinary point that a report needs an owner.

**Editing guidance:** Name the owner requirement and explain its consequence.

**Limit:** A genuine reversal or discovery may deserve a setup.


<a id="p098"></a>

### P098 — Self-answered rhetorical questions

Source records: S07,S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The passage asks an easy question only to supply an immediate answer, or stacks questions as a substitute for analysis.

**Illustrative example:** Is the process perfect? No. Does it work? Absolutely.

**What to notice:** The writer asks questions that require little thought, then supplies emphatic answers. The exchange substitutes rhythm for explanation.

**Editing guidance:** Explain the result and its limits directly.

**Limit:** FAQs and interview formats use questions for a real reader need.


<a id="p099"></a>

### P099 Announcing honesty

Source records: S07,S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The writer repeatedly announces that they are being honest or tells the reader to pay attention, without adding evidence.

**Illustrative example:** I will be honest: this changes everything. Let that sink in.

**What to notice:** “I will be honest” and “Let that sink in” ask for trust and attention. Neither supports the claim that everything has changed.

**Editing guidance:** Show the evidence or consequence that warrants attention.

**Limit:** These expressions occur naturally in speech.


<a id="p100"></a>

### P100 Pretending to know the reader

Source records: S07,S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The narrator claims to know the reader's private thoughts or invites a simulated shared experience.

**Illustrative example:** You already know what the dashboard is hiding. We have all felt it.

**What to notice:** The narrator assumes knowledge of the reader's thoughts and feelings without having been told them.

**Editing guidance:** Address the reader's actual task without inventing shared feelings.

**Limit:** Established relationships and personal essays may justify familiarity.


<a id="p101"></a>

### P101 Unasked-for emotional reassurance

Source records: S07,S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The response validates feelings or offers comfort even though the reader has not asked for emotional support.

**Illustrative example:** Your spreadsheet anxiety is real. You are not alone. Sit with that.

**What to notice:** The response treats a spreadsheet problem as an emotional experience needing validation. No one has asked for that reassurance.

**Editing guidance:** Offer relevant help; retain reassurance when the context calls for it.

**Limit:** Supportive language is appropriate in many genuine conversations.


<a id="p102"></a>

### P102 — Presenting one idea as the whole answer

Source records: S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The entire problem, the whole point or the only answer compresses a complex issue into a slogan.

**Illustrative example:** The entire strategy is trust. That is the whole game.

**What to notice:** Trust is declared to be the entire strategy. The slogan leaves out every other decision a strategy would require.

**Editing guidance:** Specify which part of the issue the claim explains.

**Limit:** Some problems genuinely have one decisive constraint.


<a id="p103"></a>

### P103 Personal endorsements with no explanation

Source records: S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A stock first-person endorsement lends authority without supplying experience or reasons.

**Illustrative example:** The only dashboard I trust is the one that makes me uncomfortable.

**What to notice:** The narrator offers a personal endorsement without describing any experience that would justify it.

**Editing guidance:** Explain the criteria used to judge the dashboard.

**Limit:** A real personal preference is not suspicious by itself.


<a id="p104"></a>

### P104 Inviting readers to verify a claim without a source

Source records: S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A request to check the claim is used as a rhetorical flourish without giving a useful way to do so.

**Illustrative example:** Do not take my word for it. The results speak for themselves.

**What to notice:** The sentence invites verification, then supplies no result or source that the reader can check.

**Editing guidance:** Provide the actual results and a usable source.

**Limit:** A specific invitation to reproduce a finding is useful.


<a id="p105"></a>

### P105 Vague promises about easy software

Source records: S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A software description promises that everything will be simple without explaining what the product does or what setup it needs.

**Illustrative example:** It fits in your head, just works and comes with batteries included.

**What to notice:** The promises suggest easy software without describing setup, limitations or actual features.

**Editing guidance:** List the supported workflow, included components and configuration defaults.

**Limit:** These idioms are common in longstanding human software documentation.


<a id="p106"></a>

### P106 Declaring one thing dead to promote another

Source records: S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A topic is declared dead to introduce its supposed successor.

**Illustrative example:** The annual plan is dead. Long live continuous planning.

**What to notice:** The first sentence announces the death of an idea; the second introduces its replacement. The drama stands in for an explanation.

**Editing guidance:** Describe the change and the circumstances in which it helps.

**Limit:** Headlines have used this construction for centuries.


<a id="p107"></a>

### P107 Tacking importance onto an ordinary event

Source records: S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The ending tells readers why a moment mattered without developing that significance in the account.

**Illustrative example:** She closed the ticket. And that is why Tuesday mattered.

**What to notice:** Closing a ticket is followed by a declaration that the day mattered. The story has not established that significance.

**Editing guidance:** Describe the effect of closing the ticket.

**Limit:** Retrospective narration is legitimate when the effect is established.


<a id="p108"></a>

### P108 — Short contrast endings

Source records: S09,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The second half of a contrast ends with a short verb such as “did,” “was” or “does.” Repeating this device gives ordinary statements a dramatic finish.

**Illustrative example:** The forecast failed. The team did not.

**What to notice:** The second sentence borrows its meaning from the first and ends abruptly. The contrast gives the statement a polished, dramatic finish.

**Editing guidance:** Use the ellipsis only when its meaning is clear and its emphasis earned.

**Limit:** This is ordinary English ellipsis, not an authorship test.


<a id="p109"></a>

### P109 Turning routine work into a life lesson

Source records: S07,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A work incident is forced into a personal growth story, humble announcement or leadership moral. Includes privilege of, taught me and not in the job description.

**Illustrative example:** I had the privilege of fixing a printer. It taught me what leadership really means.

**What to notice:** Fixing a printer becomes a lesson in leadership without an explanation of what was learned.

**Editing guidance:** Describe the incident and any specific lesson it supports.

**Limit:** Real reflection and gratitude need not be stripped from personal writing.


<a id="p110"></a>

### P110 Empty invitations to react

Source records: S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A generic request for reactions or a one-of-those opening simulates conversation while adding little information.

**Illustrative example:** It was one of those days. Curious what others think.

**What to notice:** “One of those days” assumes a shared experience without describing the day. The request for reactions has little for readers to respond to.

**Editing guidance:** State the experience; ask a question with a clear purpose if feedback is needed.

**Limit:** Useful discussion prompts and informal conversation can use these forms.


<a id="p111"></a>

### P111 — Generic scene-setting openings

Source records: S05,S07,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The opening spends time on weather, light, location or a broad era before reaching the event that matters.

**Illustrative example:** As the morning sun lit the city, a quiet transformation was taking place at the office.

**What to notice:** The morning light delays the office story, while “a quiet transformation” hints at importance without naming what happened.

**Editing guidance:** Start with the event unless the atmosphere affects it.

**Limit:** Atmospheric openings are often effective in fiction.


<a id="p112"></a>

### P112 — Predictably uplifting endings

Source records: S05,S07,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Conflict gives way to generic hope, togetherness, belonging or a tidy happy ending regardless of the preceding evidence.

**Illustrative example:** Whatever came next, they would face it together. For now, that was enough.

**What to notice:** The ending offers hope and togetherness without resolving the particular problem in the story.

**Editing guidance:** End at the actual outcome, including unresolved matters.

**Limit:** Hopeful conclusions are not inherently formulaic.


<a id="p113"></a>

### P113 Stock descriptions of fear and surprise

Source records: S03,S05,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A scene relies on familiar descriptions such as a racing heart, a shiver down the spine or time seeming to stop.

**Illustrative example:** Her heart hammered against her ribs. His words hung in the air for what felt like an eternity.

**What to notice:** A pounding heart and words that “hung in the air” are familiar shortcuts for emotion. Neither reveals much about these particular characters.

**Editing guidance:** Choose an observed action or reaction specific to the scene.

**Limit:** These are longstanding fiction clichés shared by human authors.


<a id="p114"></a>

### P114 Hinting at danger without giving a clue

Source records: S03,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The narrator promises change or danger without giving the reader a concrete clue.

**Illustrative example:** Little did he know that something darker was waiting. Nothing would ever be the same.

**What to notice:** The narrator promises danger but gives no clue about its cause or form.

**Editing guidance:** Supply a meaningful clue or let the later event establish the change.

**Limit:** Withholding information is a valid narrative technique.


<a id="p115"></a>

### P115 — Piled-on metaphors and adjectives

Source records: S03,S05,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Metaphors and adjectives pile up without making the subject easier to picture or understand.

**Illustrative example:** The vibrant, intricate tapestry of teamwork illuminated a profound journey of discovery.

**What to notice:** A tapestry is said to illuminate a journey. The piled-up images do not explain what the team did.

**Example context:** The metaphors and adjectives pile up without describing what the team did.

**Editing guidance:** Keep images that reveal something specific; remove decoration that competes with the meaning.

**Limit:** Literary prose may intentionally be ornate.

**Additional examples:**

- **An elaborate metaphor:** The budget is the heartbeat of the project, and every invoice is another pulse in its unfolding story.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p116"></a>

### P116 — Telling the reader what a scene means

Source records: S03,S05,S07. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The narrator explains an emotion or moral already apparent from the action, leaving little for the reader to infer.

**Illustrative example:** She returned his unopened letters. This showed that she no longer trusted him.

**What to notice:** Returning the letters already suggests damaged trust. The next sentence tells the reader how to interpret it.

**Editing guidance:** Let the action carry the inference unless explanation adds necessary context.

**Limit:** Explicit explanation can be appropriate for the audience or genre.


<a id="p117"></a>

### P117 Missing specific details

Source records: S03,S05. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A passage substitutes generalized descriptions for observed particulars, motives, memories or the character's own perception.

**Illustrative example:** He felt many complex emotions about the difficult situation.

**What to notice:** “Many complex emotions” and “the difficult situation” could fit almost any story. No feeling or circumstance is named.

**Editing guidance:** Identify the relevant observation or thought using facts already available.

**Limit:** Never invent personal experience or evidence to make prose sound human.


<a id="p118"></a>

### P118 — Repeated words and restated conclusions

Source records: S03,S05,S08,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The passage repeats its point, preferred words or transitions without adding evidence or a new inference.

**Illustrative example:** The launch was a success. The team achieved a successful outcome through its efforts. Overall, the project demonstrated the importance of working toward success.

**What to notice:** “Success,” “successful” and “success” repeat the same judgment. The passage supplies no result that would demonstrate it.

**Example context:** Each sentence returns to the same claim.

**Editing guidance:** Remove duplicate propositions and keep repetition that serves comprehension.

**Limit:** Technical terminology should remain consistent rather than being replaced with synonyms.


<a id="p119"></a>

### P119 General statements that say little

Source records: S08,S12. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Long passages offer statements broad enough to fit almost any topic, with little usable information.

**Illustrative example:** Many factors influence outcomes, and considering them can support better decisions.

**What to notice:** The statement would fit business, education or almost any other subject. It names no factor, outcome or decision.

**Editing guidance:** Name the factor, decision and evidence relevant to this case.

**Limit:** A high-level overview can be useful if it is the requested deliverable.


<a id="p120"></a>

### P120 — Answering the topic but missing the task

Source records: S08,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The text discusses the broad subject while failing to address the reader's question, implied intent or requested level of detail.

**Illustrative example:** Question: How can I reduce our weekly meeting from an hour to thirty minutes?<br>Answer: Meetings play a crucial role in workplace communication. They provide an opportunity for collaboration and help teams stay aligned.

**What to notice:** The question asks how to shorten a meeting. The answer explains why meetings exist and gives no practical step.

**Example context:** The answer describes meetings but gives no way to shorten one.

**Editing guidance:** Identify the decision or action the reader needs and answer it.

**Limit:** Scope misunderstandings can happen in human writing too.


<a id="p121"></a>

### P121 Smooth transitions between unrelated ideas

Source records: S03,S08. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Sentences sound related but do not develop a coherent argument or consistent narrative.

**Illustrative example:** The server is slow. Furthermore, customers value trust. Consequently, the office needs more light.

**What to notice:** “Furthermore” and “Consequently” make the sentences sound connected. Nothing explains how server speed leads to office lighting.

**Editing guidance:** Check the logical link between each claim and the next.

**Limit:** A rough draft is not evidence of machine authorship.


<a id="p122"></a>

### P122 Confusing wording or tense

Source records: S03,S08. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

An odd word choice, unclear pronoun or unexplained tense change makes a sentence difficult to understand.

**Illustrative example:** Maya called Noor after she left yesterday, and tomorrow she was waiting.

**What to notice:** “She” could mean Maya or Noor, and “tomorrow she was waiting” shifts between future and past.

**Editing guidance:** Clarify who acts, use the intended time frame and choose idiomatic wording.

**Limit:** Translation, language learning and ordinary drafting can produce the same problems.


<a id="p123"></a>

### P123 Everyone quoted sounds the same

Source records: S05. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Different speakers sound like the narrator or each other, with identical polish, vocabulary and syntax.

**Illustrative example:** “This is a transformative opportunity for our community,” said the headteacher.<br>“This is a transformative opportunity for local businesses,” said the shopkeeper.<br>“This is a transformative opportunity for young people,” said the student.

**What to notice:** Three different speakers use almost identical praise. Their individual voices and reasons are missing.

**Example context:** Unrelated speakers use almost identical phrasing.

**Editing guidance:** Check the recording or source and preserve each speaker's wording.

**Limit:** Do not rewrite a direct quotation merely to make it sound less polished.


<a id="p124"></a>

### P124 — A tone that does not fit the situation

Source records: S05,S08,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The voice stays formal, detached or promotional in contexts requiring a different tone; contractions, slang and ordinary abbreviations disappear.

**Illustrative example:** Please be advised that our household milk supply has been depleted. Your attention to replenishment would be greatly appreciated.

**What to notice:** A request to buy milk sounds like a formal notice. The wording does not fit an ordinary conversation between housemates.

**Example context:** A message to a flatmate uses the voice of a formal notice.

**Editing guidance:** Match language to the audience and purpose.

**Limit:** Formal language alone is an ineffective indicator; compare P074.

**Additional examples:**

- **Avoiding contractions:** It is time to review the plan. We do not need another meeting. There is no reason to wait.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p125"></a>

### P125 Making every experience sound positive

Source records: S05,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Every situation is handled with neutral or positive phrasing, without the humour, discomfort, anger or ambiguity the subject would warrant.

**Illustrative example:** The argument left the siblings with valuable opportunities for growth and a deeper appreciation of their shared journey.

**What to notice:** The family argument becomes a cheerful opportunity for growth. The wording avoids the hurt or disagreement the situation would normally involve.

**Example context:** A painful family dispute is smoothed into cheerful, generic language.

**Editing guidance:** Represent the event accurately and allow the relevant emotional range.

**Limit:** Profanity, dark subjects and jokes are not prerequisites for human writing.


<a id="p126"></a>

### P126 — Giving both sides equal weight by default

Source records: S05,S08,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The text presents equal sides mechanically or avoids a justified perspective. The opposite problem is a confident one-sided claim without support.

**Illustrative example:** The delivery was due on Monday and arrived on Friday. Both sides offer equally valid perspectives on whether it was late.

**What to notice:** The dates establish that the delivery was late. The appeal to “equally valid perspectives” creates an unnecessary balance.

**Example context:** The second sentence manufactures uncertainty despite the dates already given.

**Editing guidance:** Represent uncertainty and competing evidence in proportion to their support.

**Limit:** Impartial reporting and balanced analysis remain valuable.


<a id="p127"></a>

### P127 Descriptions that could fit any place

Source records: S05,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Examples and stories avoid specific cultural references, personal observations, callbacks or surprising but relevant details.

**Illustrative example:** Our neighborhood is a place where friendly people come together. Its welcoming streets reflect a strong sense of community and a shared commitment to the future.

**What to notice:** Friendly people and welcoming streets could describe almost any neighborhood. Nothing identifies this one.

**Example context:** Nothing identifies this neighborhood or distinguishes it from another.

**Editing guidance:** Use relevant, supportable particulars instead of interchangeable scenery.

**Limit:** A text need not be quirky or autobiographical to be good.


<a id="p128"></a>

### P128 — Repeated full names and honorifics

Source records: S05. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The same person is repeatedly introduced by full name or title, or every authority is dressed in the same honorific formula.

**Illustrative example:** Dr. Maya Patel spoke. Dr. Maya Patel explained the chart. Dr. Maya Patel then left.

**What to notice:** The full name and title repeat in every sentence, even though the reader already knows who is being discussed.

**Editing guidance:** After a clear introduction, use the appropriate shorter reference.

**Limit:** Professional and legal conventions may require repetition. Name ethnicity is not an acceptable authorship cue.


<a id="p129"></a>

### P129 — Nouns piled on nouns

Source records: S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Actions are turned into abstract nouns and stacked together. It becomes hard to see who is doing what.

**Illustrative example:** Implementation of the optimisation requires coordination of the evaluation of the integration.

**What to notice:** “Implementation,” “coordination,” “evaluation” and “integration” turn actions into a chain of nouns. It is hard to see who needs to do what.

**Editing guidance:** Name the actor and use a verb where it improves clarity.

**Limit:** Technical and legal nouns often carry necessary precision.


<a id="p130"></a>

### P130 Punctuation that makes the passage hard to follow

Source records: S05,S11,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Sentences repeatedly leave out helpful punctuation or use the same punctuation pattern regardless of their meaning.

**Illustrative example:** The proposal which the team discussed last week and which the manager revised after the meeting will go ahead if the budget that finance approved is still available.

**What to notice:** Several long qualifications interrupt the main point without punctuation to guide the reader.

**Example context:** Several interruptions are left unmarked, making the sentence harder to follow.

**Editing guidance:** Punctuate for meaning; examine the passage rather than counting one symbol.

**Limit:** The sources disagree on punctuation direction. Treat these as corpus-specific measurements.

**Additional examples:**

- **The same punctuation throughout:** The hotel offers a pool and a restaurant. Guests can visit the beach. The rooms provide comfortable accommodation. The area has several attractions.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p131"></a>

### P131 — Sentences too long for their content

Source records: S03,S05,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Long clause chains or run-on sentences obscure the main claim.

**Illustrative example:** The team, which reviewed the logs, which had arrived late, which complicated the review, approved the change.

**What to notice:** Three “which” clauses delay the main action: the team approved the change.

**Editing guidance:** Separate distinct claims and preserve necessary dependencies.

**Limit:** Sentence length depends strongly on genre, language and intended readership.


<a id="p132"></a>

### P132 — Making abstractions act like people

Source records: S07. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Abstractions perform actions that obscure who made the decision or what caused the outcome.

**Illustrative example:** The strategy decided to embrace a new operating model.

**What to notice:** A strategy cannot make a decision. The sentence hides the people who chose the operating model.

**Editing guidance:** Name the decision maker or state the actual mechanism.

**Limit:** Personification is legitimate when it helps rather than obscures.


<a id="p133"></a>

### P133 Claims about everyone, always or never

Source records: S07. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Every, always, never or nobody is used for rhetorical force where the evidence supports a narrower statement.

**Illustrative example:** Nobody reads reports. Every successful team uses dashboards.

**What to notice:** “Nobody” and “every” make universal claims about people and teams without evidence for either.

**Editing guidance:** Restrict the claim to the population and evidence available.

**Limit:** Universal claims are sometimes demonstrably true.


<a id="p134"></a>

### P134 Announcing what the document will say

Source records: S07,S11,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The writer describes what the article will discuss or how the reader should follow it instead of advancing the subject.

**Illustrative example:** In this section, we will explore the points that will be discussed below.

**What to notice:** The sentence announces what will be discussed instead of beginning the discussion.

**Editing guidance:** Keep navigation that helps with a long document; delete redundant previews.

**Limit:** A roadmap can be useful in a dissertation or complex report.


<a id="p135"></a>

### P135 — Trying to address everyone at once

Source records: S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

Whether-you-are openings or think-of-it-as metaphors promise universal relevance without examining the audience.

**Illustrative example:** Whether you are a beginner or an expert, think of the budget as a compass.

**What to notice:** The opening addresses beginners and experts together, then uses a familiar compass metaphor. It gives neither group specific help.

**Editing guidance:** Specify who needs the advice and explain the actual mechanism.

**Limit:** A well-chosen analogy can make a difficult idea accessible.


<a id="p136"></a>

### P136 — A caveat followed by automatic reassurance

Source records: S07,S11. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A small concession is immediately followed by reassurance, making the qualification feel obligatory.

**Illustrative example:** It is not perfect, but it is a powerful step forward.

**What to notice:** The small concession is immediately followed by praise. The reader learns neither what is imperfect nor why it is a step forward.

**Editing guidance:** Explain the specific limitation and the demonstrated benefit.

**Limit:** Concession and qualification are normal reasoning tools.


<a id="p137"></a>

### P137 The same group of words keeps appearing

Source records: S11,S13. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

A passage draws repeatedly on a particular group of words. A pattern across the whole passage can be more revealing than any single word, especially when comparing writing on the same subject.

**Illustrative example:** The boundary is load-bearing. The fallback silently absorbs the mismatch. The invariant remains intact, but the abstraction leaks at the call site.

**What to notice:** The passage packs together terms from software development. Many have precise technical uses; the observation concerns a repeated cluster, not a banned-word list.

**Example context:** The interest is in the concentration of vocabulary. Each term can also be correct technical language.

**Editing guidance:** Use the measurement only within its validated population, if any. Review actual meaning before editing.

**Limit:** A fitted list is not a banned-word list and does not establish authorship.


<a id="p138"></a>

### P138 Too many linking phrases

Source records: S05,S07,S11,S14. Practitioner heuristic unless a study-specific qualification is given in the evidence notes below.

The passage repeatedly inserts a transition regardless of whether it expresses a real relationship.

**Illustrative example:** Furthermore, the plan supports growth. Additionally, it encourages progress. Moreover, it creates opportunities. Ultimately, these benefits demonstrate its value.

**What to notice:** Each sentence begins with another transition. The claims about growth and progress remain vague and repetitive.

**Example context:** Transitions keep arriving, but the claims remain broad and repetitive.

**Editing guidance:** Use a transition only where it identifies the logical relationship.

**Limit:** An isolated transition is weak evidence; see P075.

**Additional examples:**

- **Looking ahead:** Looking ahead, the organization is poised for a new chapter of growth.
- **What comes next:** What comes next will shape the future of how we work together.
- **Another layer:** The decision adds another layer to an already complex conversation.
- **Another dimension:** Community spirit adds another dimension to the story of this event.
- **In practice:** In practice, this approach creates opportunities for meaningful progress.
- **Together these:** Together these ideas provide a foundation for lasting success.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.

## Evidence, disagreements and limits

### Research about quality versus authorship

LAMP (S03-S04) supplies seven editing categories from creative-writing work: cliché, unnecessary explanation, excess ornament, poor structure, missing detail, awkward phrasing and tense inconsistency. These identify editing needs, not necessarily machine origin. Measuring Slop (S08) distinguishes information accuracy and perspective, density and relevance, repetition, templates, coherence, naturalness, verbosity, vocabulary complexity and tone. A human draft can also have these problems.

### Early ChatGPT findings are historically bounded

HC3 (S14-S15) compares human and early ChatGPT answers. Its observed differences include answer length, vocabulary diversity, parts of speech, punctuation and dependency structure. In English, the study reports more nouns, verbs, determiners, adjectives, auxiliaries, coordinating conjunctions and particles, and fewer adverbs and punctuation tokens in ChatGPT answers. These are corpus distributions, not instructions to count a student's determiners. Perplexity depends on the scoring model; it is not a visible prose rule.

### Human Detectors: retain observations without its absolutes

The guide and coding taxonomy (S05-S06) preserve expert observations. Claims that dialect, grammar or certain punctuation almost always identify a human are not adopted here. The guide associates actually and really with human writing while other lists flag them as AI-like filler. It lists and that was that among human-associated language while also criticising tidy closure. Evaluate the function and the surrounding passage.

### Names and identity

The Human Detectors guide lists Alex, Amanda, Emily, Emma, Grant, Jessica, John, Laura, Liam, Lisa, Marcus, Mark, Max, Michael, Rachel, Sarah, Sophia and Tom; and Chen, Daniels, Johnson, Lee, Patel, Reynolds, Roberts, Rodriguez, Smith and Thompson. It also speculates about combinations of names from different cultural traditions. We record that claim for coverage but reject name ethnicity or an ordinary name as an authorship test. Repeated introductions and implausibly uniform attribution are the editorial issues captured in P128.

### Stop Slop is a house style

S07 recommends removing all adverbs, passive constructions and em dashes; preferring two items over three; avoiding Wh-, So- and Look-openers; and changing equal-length sentence runs. These are preferences rather than validated detection rules. Its directness, rhythm, trust, authenticity and density rubric uses five ten-point ratings and a 35/50 revision threshold. No validated authorship calibration is supplied. Its own rewrites sometimes retain contrasts, dashes or sweeping claims, which reinforces the need for judgment.

### Linter thresholds and inconsistent prose

The linter uses single-match alerts, density checks and repeated-frame checks. Its comma scarcity cutoff is at most 20 per 1,000 words; its sentence-ending proxy is at most 20 per 1,000. It uses noun-suffix matches as a nominalisation proxy. These approximations have false positives. The two breadth rules use distinct matches divided by word count to the power 0.7, with thresholds 0.31 and 0.095 respectively, and minimums of 600 words, five sentences and ten matches. Some accompanying notes describe a square root or older calibration values; the inspected configuration is recorded in the concordance. No runtime behavior was tested.

### Vocabulary changes are aggregate evidence

## Additional patterns from the supplied Graphite review

<a id="p139"></a>

### P139 Repeatedly saying that something matters

Source records: S22 and S23; user-supplied additions reviewed 2 October 2026.

The writer repeatedly announces that a point matters before explaining its consequence. Bare importance statements, rankings and declarations that a distinction matters can add emphasis without adding information.

**Illustrative example:** This matters: the launch has moved to Friday. Just as important, the team has another day to prepare. That distinction matters. Ultimately, what matters most is being ready.

**What to notice:** The passage repeatedly says that something matters. The repeated announcements add little to the concrete point that the launch moved.

**Editing guidance:** State the consequence directly. Keep an importance flag when it helps the reader identify a real priority.

**Limit:** A consequence, priority or comparison can justify this wording. Frequency alone does not make a statement wrong.

**Additional examples:**

- **This matters:** This matters. The meeting is now on Friday.
- **Why this matters:** Why this matters is that the team has more time to prepare.
- **Matters most:** What matters most is our shared commitment to doing better.
- **Just as important:** Just as important is the opportunity to grow together.
- **The distinction matters:** The meeting was delayed, not canceled. The distinction matters.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p140"></a>

### P140 — Promises without tradeoffs

Source records: S22 and S23; user-supplied additions reviewed 2 October 2026.

A benefit is paired with a promise that nothing valuable is lost or required. Repeated assurances about preserving quality, coverage or convenience can hide the conditions under which the promise holds.

**Illustrative example:** Our platform cuts costs without sacrificing quality, accelerates delivery without compromising reliability, and simplifies your work without requiring you to change a thing.

**What to notice:** Three benefits are paired with assurances that nothing will be lost or required. The passage never explains how those promises can all hold.

**Editing guidance:** Name the benefit, the cost and the conditions. Keep a claim of no loss only when evidence supports it.

**Limit:** Some changes really do remove a requirement or preserve a capability. Check the claim rather than banning the construction.

**Additional examples:**

- **Without sacrificing:** The new service reduces costs without sacrificing quality.
- **Without compromising:** Our approach delivers results without compromising your values.
- **Without losing:** The redesign embraces the future without losing the spirit of the past.
- **Without requiring:** The system transforms your routine without requiring any extra effort.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p141"></a>

### P141 Saying something is helpful without explaining why

Source records: S22 and S23; user-supplied additions reviewed 2 October 2026.

The prose repeatedly describes a tool or step as helpful, easy or useful to the reader. The claim can substitute for an explanation of what the tool does and when it helps.

**Illustrative example:** The dashboard is a helpful tool for your team. Its useful features make it easier to work effectively, helping you achieve better results with greater ease.

**What to notice:** “Helpful,” “useful” and “easier” repeat a benefit without identifying what the dashboard actually does.

**Editing guidance:** Describe the action or result. Retain a helpfulness claim when the benefit and its conditions are clear.

**Limit:** Instructional writing often needs to explain benefits. These phrases can be appropriate and accurate.

**Additional examples:**

- **Can help you:** This helpful guide can help you achieve more meaningful results.
- **Especially helpful:** The framework is especially helpful for navigating complex challenges.
- **Makes it easier:** The tool makes it easier to work more effectively.
- **Helps you avoid:** The checklist helps you avoid common obstacles on your journey.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p142"></a>

### P142 Too many qualifications and reassurances

Source records: S22 and S23; user-supplied additions reviewed 2 October 2026.

The writer keeps adding qualifications and reassurance after the uncertainty is already clear. Repeated “may,” “not necessarily” and “does not establish” clauses can bury the actual point.

**Illustrative example:** The result may suggest an improvement, though it does not necessarily establish one. This is not to say the change was ineffective, only that its effectiveness remains uncertain. That said, it may still offer value, even if that value is not yet fully clear.

**What to notice:** The first sentence already makes the uncertainty clear. The following qualifications circle around it without adding information.

**Editing guidance:** Match the strength of the statement to the evidence. Explain a relevant uncertainty once; remove only redundant qualification.

**Limit:** Scientific, legal and technical writing often requires careful qualification. Do not turn an uncertain claim into a certainty to avoid a suspected tell.

**Additional examples:**

- **Does not establish:** This result does not establish that the new approach caused the improvement.
- **May provide:** The change may provide an opportunity for further progress.
- **Can provide:** The framework can provide a useful foundation for future discussions.
- **Not necessarily:** A positive result is not necessarily evidence of lasting success.
- **Need not:** The reader need not feel that every question must be resolved immediately.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.


<a id="p143"></a>

### P143 — Unsupported superlatives

Source records: S22 and S23; user-supplied additions reviewed 2 October 2026.

The writer repeatedly calls something the best, most powerful or most popular without defining the comparison. Words such as arguably or perhaps can soften the ranking without supplying evidence.

**Illustrative example:** This is the single most common cause of the retry storm.

**What to notice:** “The single most common” asserts a ranking. The sentence provides no comparison or measurement to support it.

**Editing guidance:** Specify the comparison and evidence, or replace the ranking with a concrete property.

**Limit:** A measured ranking can be precise. Hedging a ranking is appropriate when the comparison is genuinely uncertain.

**Additional examples:**

- **The single most:** This is the single most important decision your team will make.
- **Arguably the most:** This is arguably the most effective approach available today.
- **The most powerful:** Trust is the most powerful tool in any leader’s toolkit.
- **The most popular:** This is the most popular way to bring teams together.
- **Perhaps the most:** This is perhaps the most significant shift in modern working life.
- **One of the best:** This is one of the best books about leadership.

Source records: S22 and S23; supplied variants merged into this entry.

## Editorial review of sources October 2026

All 142 retained entries were reviewed for a readable description and an example that demonstrates the observation. Formatting specimens use line breaks and visible markup where that is what a reader would encounter. Context is separated from the example. The empty-heading entry was removed at the owner's request; remaining entry identifiers were not renumbered.

All 23 registered sources were revisited online. Wikipedia, Vale, Stop Slop and the cliché highlighter describe patterns and examples; they do not provide a human-relative frequency for each listed phrase. Human Detectors records reviewers' observations. Its overall detection results are not usage rates for individual words. The creative-writing alignment study supplies an editing taxonomy and explicitly says its formative study was not intended to establish the relative prevalence of categories. Measuring AI Slop supplies annotation categories and experimental measurements, not a universal word-frequency lookup. HC3 compares early ChatGPT answers with human answers across languages and domains; its vocabulary-diversity measures must not be treated as rates for each word. MAGE and M4 are detection benchmarks, not prose rulebooks. The Measuring Slop companion repository did not provide a separate usable vocabulary inventory.

The two Graphite articles provide directly relevant, model-specific usage ratios. The biomedical excess-vocabulary study provides historical changes in abstracts. Selected measurements are included above with their comparison populations. The broad code-review lists remain available as source inventories, with their scope explained: common words and technical tokens are not standalone signs. No external repository, skill file, model or package was installed or executed during this review.

The 47 supplied additional forms remain covered. Five descriptions previously labeled as examples were replaced with original prose that demonstrates the same form. Source examples and new illustrations must not be treated as output sampled for a controlled experiment.
